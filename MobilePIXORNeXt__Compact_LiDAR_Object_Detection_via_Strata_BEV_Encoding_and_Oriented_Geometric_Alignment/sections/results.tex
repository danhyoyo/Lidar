\subsection{Inference Latency and Real-Time Complexity}
\label{sec:latency_analysis}
\input{tables/efficiency}

\paragraph{Pipeline Latency vs. Model Forward Throughput.}
Network time covers only part of the offline pipeline. Input preparation, H2D transfer, decoding, and NMS contribute additional latency. Two archived reports are available, with different GPUs and slightly different AP results:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{L4 model stage}: $8.67\,\text{ms}$ ($115.32\,\text{FPS}$) in the seed-42 L4 evaluation report. This value is not a standalone T4 measurement.
    \item \textbf{T4 model stage}: $17.88\,\text{ms}$ ($55.94\,\text{FPS}$) within the separate T4 profiling snapshot, whose offline pipeline totals $60.96\,\text{ms}$.
\end{enumerate}
Table~\ref{tab:efficiency} retains GPU identity and the measured network-stage boundary. The T4 rows use batch size 1 and 3,759 timed frames after 10 warmup frames. The historical MobilePIXOR row is from L4 and must not be used for a same-hardware speedup estimate.



\textit{1. End-to-End Pipeline Latency Comparison.}
The T4 profiling snapshot records $60.96\,\text{ms}$ ($16.41\,\text{FPS}$) for MobilePIXORNeXt and $63.84\,\text{ms}$ ($15.66\,\text{FPS}$) for PointPillars. Their median latencies are $58.28\,\text{ms}$ and $62.10\,\text{ms}$. MobilePIXORNeXt has higher P95/P99 values ($80.85/95.91\,\text{ms}$) than PointPillars ($75.88/92.73\,\text{ms}$). Both archived P99 values are below $100\,\text{ms}$, but these offline statistics neither establish a worst-case deadline nor validate latency in closed-loop driving. No scene-density stratification is available to assign a cause to the tail.

\textit{2. Component Latency Profiling and Host-to-Device Efficiency.}
The four profiled T4 stage means sum to $60.67\,\text{ms}$; the difference from the directly measured $60.96\,\text{ms}$ pipeline mean is $0.29\,\text{ms}$. This is a measurement residual, not a separately profiled metadata-processing stage:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input Preprocessing ($20.94\,\text{ms}$)}: Point-cloud file loading and Strata-BEV14 rasterization with the Numba CPU backend took $20.94\,\text{ms}$ combined. The implementation uses occupied-cell accumulators and precomputed logarithmic lookup tables. Recorded preprocessing took $27.75\,\text{ms}$ for PointPillars and $27.20\,\text{ms}$ for SECOND. These measurements cover complete input preparation from disk to memory.
    \item \textbf{Host-to-Device Transfer ($6.91\,\text{ms}$)}: The $800\times704\times14$ FP32 tensor requires $31.54\,\text{MB}$, compared with $78.85\,\text{MB}$ for 35 channels, a $60.0\%$ payload reduction. The historical Binary35 report records $40.63\,\text{ms}$ transfer time on L4. Hardware, memory pinning, and implementation differ, so the timing ratio cannot isolate the benefit of channel reduction.
    \item \textbf{Model Stage ($17.88\,\text{ms}$)}: The T4 report records $55.94\,\text{FPS}$ for this stage. The separate $8.67\,\text{ms}$ value is from L4; no same-GPU isolated-versus-pipeline experiment establishes the cause of their difference.
    \item \textbf{Decoding and Rotated NMS ($14.94\,\text{ms}$)}: This is the combined post-processing stage. It does not separately measure peak extraction, coordinate decoding, NMS, and output transfer.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Historical Configuration Inventory.}
Table~\ref{tab:components} now records source run identities alongside the historical configurations. The records change multiple factors, training budgets, and selection criteria. They document the experimental trajectory rather than a controlled additive decomposition of the final detector.

\paragraph{1. Historical Backbone Configurations (A0, A1).}
The archived B0 and B1 results are $41.40\%$ and $47.60\%$ Moderate mAP. B0 was archived at 38 of 100 planned epochs and uses an epoch-30 checkpoint; B1 completed 100 epochs and uses a loss-selected epoch-13 checkpoint. The $6.20\,\text{pp}$ difference is descriptive and cannot be attributed solely to replacing residual blocks.

\paragraph{2. Historical BEV Representations (A1--A3).}
\input{tables/encoding}
Table~\ref{tab:encoding} reports the early representation experiments. Strata-BEV14 reaches $50.14\%$ Moderate mAP, compared with $47.60\%$ for Binary35, $47.37\%$ for Strata-BEV8, and $44.42\%$ for the local TriBand-BEV adaptation. The 14-channel payload is $31.54\,\text{MB}$ versus $78.85\,\text{MB}$ for Binary35. Because Binary35 uses a different training budget and checkpoint-selection criterion, the $2.54\,\text{pp}$ AP difference is not a controlled estimate of the representation effect. These comparisons also change groups of input features together rather than isolating centroid offsets or elevation moments.

\paragraph{3. Historical Head and Sampler Configurations (A3--A5).}
The grouped-H16 configuration G16 records $51.30\%$ Moderate mAP versus $50.14\%$ for the earlier single-head E14 configuration. The $60.22\%$ result assigned to A5 is O32, which uses grouped H32 heads, F32 fusion, and a simplified GT sampler. It changes augmentation and neck width as well as head width relative to G16. Accordingly, the $8.92\,\text{pp}$ difference does not establish a head-capacity effect, and no rank or gradient analysis demonstrates latent subspace collapse.

\paragraph{4. Historical Sampler Recipes (A5--A7).}
The result $66.25\%$ Moderate mAP belongs to O32G16: a simplified-sampler V2 run with H16 heads and F32 SG-FPN. It is not an H32/F24 PC-GTA addition to O32. The historical O32 and H32 runs share the recorded architecture and score $60.22\%$ and $68.12\%$, but also change sampler probability, quotas, and placement settings. Their $7.91\,\text{pp}$ difference therefore compares complete sampler recipes and does not isolate ground fitting, obstacle rejection, or range subsampling.

\paragraph{5. Neck and Context Configurations (A7, A8, A8F).}
H32 records $68.12\%$ Moderate mAP using SumFPN, while S32 records $68.46\%$ using SG-FPN. Their insertion quotas also differ (6/8/10 versus 15/10/10), so the $0.34\,\text{pp}$ difference is not an isolated neck effect. The previously assigned A8 score of $67.38\%$ belongs instead to P1, which adds focal context. Table~\ref{tab:components} identifies it separately as A8F.

\paragraph{6. Historical Geometric Supervision (A8F, A9).}
Within the earlier focal-context architecture, P1 and P4 score $67.38\%$ and $70.58\%$ Moderate mAP. This $3.20\,\text{pp}$ difference combines OGA and the IQA prediction/supervision/ranking branch. It does not demonstrate a specific interaction with SG-FPN or establish the gain of the same package in the final shared-head model.

\paragraph{7. Final Block-SimAM Configuration (A10).}
The final shared-head seed-42 detector with Block-SimAM records $71.50\%$ Moderate mAP and $73.45\%$ $\text{mAP}_9$ in the L4 report. Its difference from historical P4 ($70.58\%$) is $0.92\,\text{pp}$, but P4 retains focal context and earlier independent towers and uses a different training budget. This is not an isolated Block-SimAM gain.
Block-SimAM itself adds no learned attention parameters. A matched final-architecture run with only Block-SimAM disabled is needed to quantify its accuracy and latency contribution; no such comparison has been established by the listed records.
The historical placement results in Table~\ref{tab:attention} also change neck width or configuration history. Their AP and speed differences are therefore not controlled placement effects.

\paragraph{Multi-Task Optimization Dynamics and Convergence.}
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_curves} shows archived loss trajectories over the first 50 epochs. The historical figure retains its original configuration labels, which require reconciliation with the run identities in Table~\ref{tab:components}. It is a qualitative view of optimization traces rather than a matched component experiment.
The displayed losses generally decrease, with transient validation fluctuations. A downward trend does not establish asymptotic convergence, and transient spikes are incompatible with describing every trajectory as monotonic. Because the configurations and task objectives differ, total loss magnitudes cannot be used to rank detector quality across runs.
T-SBUW constrains the sum and range of task weights and calibrates running loss scales. The trajectories do not isolate its effect from augmentation, architecture, or schedule. A controlled weighting ablation and gradient telemetry would be needed to attribute numerical stability to T-SBUW.

Although their aggregate AP is similar, their per-class profiles differ. The $25.0\%$ latency difference is observational; no matched candidate-count or timing experiment assigns it to heatmap sharpening, orientation calibration, or training duration alone.

\subsection{Discussion and Practical Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion_limitations}

\paragraph{Discussion and Insights.}
The local evaluation supports compact oriented BEV footprint detection without learned point encoders or 3D sparse convolutions. Strata-BEV14 retains point-cloud statistics for a detector with fewer than $0.76\,\text{M}$ deployable parameters. Historical results motivate grouped heads, physical augmentation, and geometric supervision, but their confounded settings do not support an additive attribution of the final accuracy to individual components. The empirical scope is BEV detection; complete 3D cuboid detection and closed-loop safety are not evaluated.

\paragraph{Limitations and Future Scope.}
MobilePIXORNeXt predicts horizontal footprints and orientation modulo $\pi$, without vertical extent, directed heading, tracking, or time-to-collision estimation. The empirical study uses KITTI 64-beam LiDAR, leaving sparse sensors, adverse weather, non-planar roads, and embedded platforms untested. Local and reference evaluator outputs require an equivalence study before a common accuracy ranking is justified. Matched component runs, fixed-budget seed repetitions, consistent checkpoint selection, and verified hardware/timing boundaries are needed to resolve the remaining experimental attribution questions. Future work will examine these controls and hardware-specific deployment.
