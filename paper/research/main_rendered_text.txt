Abstract—Compact LiDAR perception requires balancing spa￾tial representation, oriented localization, and detection confi￾dence. We present MobilePixorNeXt, a detector that combines
a fourteen-channel bird’s eye view representation with large￾kernel depthwise residual blocks, scale-gated feature fusion,
grouped prediction heads, and an oriented geometry objective.
Localization quality is supervised through a detached projection￾based geometric similarity, while focal context and residual
spatial attention provide optional feature refinement. A factorial
study on the KITTI validation split examines how the geometry
objective and a separate quality head interact. Under the local
bird’s eye view evaluation protocol, the quality head improves
mean average precision for Moderate objects by 2.93 percentage
points with the baseline objective and by 1.61 points with the
geometry objective, indicating overlapping benefits. The strongest
fifty-epoch variant achieves 70.99% mean average precision
with 852,930 detector parameters. A separately trained three￾dimensional variant shares an initial convolution within each task
group and predicts vertical location and height. With 797,382
parameters, it achieves 71.09% bird’s eye view and 57.75%
three-dimensional mean average precision using the pinned
reference evaluator. Conditional geometric diagnostics identify
vertical location error as a major source of failed car overlaps.
These single-seed results support compact LiDAR detection and
motivate joint consideration of geometry, confidence, and vertical
accuracy; local ablation scores and reference evaluation scores
are reported under their respective protocols.
Index Terms—LiDAR, object detection, autonomous vehicles,
bird’s eye view, localization quality.
I. INTRODUCTION
L
IDAR object detection supplies metric scene information
for autonomous vehicles and mobile robots. The detector
must recognize objects with different footprints and orienta￾tions from point clouds whose sampling varies with range and
visibility. Bird’s eye view (BEV) representations make this
problem accessible to efficient two-dimensional convolutional
networks. PIXOR established dense, proposal-free oriented
detection from BEV inputs [1], and PointPillars showed that
learned pillar features can support fast detection with a two￾dimensional backbone [2]. These approaches motivate a com￾pact architecture that retains useful vertical statistics while
allocating most learned processing to the horizontal plane.
Reducing parameter count leaves several design questions
unresolved. A richer representation can preserve information
discarded by a simple occupancy grid, but its benefit depends
on the downstream model. Multi-scale context can strengthen
feature processing, yet the resulting detections also depend on
the regression objective and their ranking scores. A classifica￾tion score alone does not explicitly encode localization quality.
Conversely, a quality score learned from a footprint does
not measure vertical overlap when the detector predicts full
three-dimensional boxes. These relationships make component
interactions relevant to the design of a small detector.
IRBGHR-PIXOR combines inverted residual blocks, Gaus￾sian heatmap regression, and a modified focal objective for
indoor pedestrian perception [3]. The present study addresses
outdoor, three-class KITTI detection and develops a different
architecture and geometric objective. Its Car, Pedestrian, and
Cyclist results therefore answer a different evaluation question
from IRBGHR-PIXOR’s JRDB pedestrian results. We retain
the useful connection between compact convolutions and
Gaussian center supervision, while examining how geometry,
quality estimation, and contextual refinement work together.
We introduce MobilePixorNeXt with a deterministic
fourteen-channel representation, a convolutional hierarchy us￾ing 7 × 7 depthwise spatial mixing, scale-gated top-down fu￾sion, and task groups for Car and Pedestrian/Cyclist. Oriented
geometric alignment (OGA) combines a multi-axis projection
objective with a footprint-scale-normalized corner distance and
adaptive task weighting. A separate quality head, referred to
as IQA in the implementation, predicts a detached geometric
similarity and participates in detection scoring. We investigate
these choices through an explicit 2×2 OGA–IQA comparison,
context and attention comparisons, checkpoint sensitivity, and
runtime decomposition.
The study also evaluates a trained three-dimensional exten￾sion with shared head convolutions and a vertical regression
branch. This extension provides a reference-evaluator result in
addition to the local BEV ablation study. A conditional anal￾ysis of fixed BEV matches examines why adequate footprints
can fail three-dimensional overlap thresholds. The principal
contributions are the compact detector implementation, the
observed interaction between geometric training and explicit
quality prediction, and an evidence-based account of vertical
localization limits. All reported runs use one random seed, and
the numerical comparisons are confined to the protocols and
training budgets under which they were obtained.

B. Convolutional context and feature fusion
Inverted residual structures [8] and modern large-kernel
ConvNets [9] motivate efficient separation of spatial mixing
and channel transformation. Our blocks use a depthwise
convolution followed by channel expansion, SiLU, projection,
batch normalization, and a scaled residual. They constitute
a specific LiDAR configuration rather than an unchanged
MobileNetV2 or ConvNeXt block. Feature pyramid networks
provide the basis for top-down fusion [10]. We use bilinear
upsampling and a depthwise gate on each lateral feature. Focal
modulation motivates hierarchical context aggregation and el￾ementwise query modulation [11]; our context module instead
uses batch normalization, dilated depthwise levels, softmax
gates, and a narrow projection. ECA [12] and SimAM [13]
supply alternative local refinements whose effects are mea￾sured individually.
C. Geometry objectives and localization quality
Focal loss addresses the dominance of easy background
examples in dense detection [14]. Gaussian center supervision,
used in Objects as Points [15], provides the classification
target in this detector. Generalized IoU supplies geometric
supervision beyond coordinate errors [16]. MGIoU extends
this idea by comparing projections of convex shapes along
their normals [17]. The implemented OGA objective uses
a BEV rectangle specialization of this projection principle
and a normalized corner term. Projection-based geometry is
attributed to MGIoU rather than claimed as a new overlap
formulation.
Generalized Focal Loss couples localization quality with
classification [18], while uncertainty-based multi-task learning
illustrates the relevance of loss balancing [19]. Our main IQA
configuration retains a separate quality branch. Its target is
a clamped mean projection GIoU, not exact polygon IoU or
three-dimensional IoU. The implemented adaptive weights are
bounded softmax task weights with running scale normaliza￾tion; they do not estimate the homoscedastic uncertainties of
Kendall et al. We evaluate the complete OGA training package
and the quality branch through matched configurations, with￾out attributing the observed gains to individual loss terms.

III. METHOD
A. Deterministic BEV representation
The input is a LiDAR scan of coordinates (x, y, z) and
reflectance. We retain finite points inside x ∈ [0, 70.4] m,
y ∈ [−40, 40] m, and z ∈ [−2.5, 1.0] m, with a small
boundary exclusion used by the encoder. Horizontal cells
measure 0.1 × 0.1 m, giving a channel-first tensor of size
14 × 800 × 704. The fourteen-channel representation, termed
Hist14, comprises four height-bin counts; maximum, mean,
span, and standard deviation of height; maximum and mean
reflectance; total density; occupancy; and two mean horizontal
offsets from the cell center. Empty cells are zero. Height
statistics use u = (z + 2.5)/3.5, and the four histogram
bins divide this normalized interval equally. Each count n is
mapped to min{1, log(1+n)/ log(33)}. Reflectance is clipped
to [0, 1], and the offsets are divided by cell width and clipped to
[−0.5, 0.5]. This fixed encoding preserves vertical summaries
without a learned point encoder. Supplementary Note S1 gives
the channel definitions and supplied channel visualization.
B. Backbone and scale-gated fusion
The stem applies two 3 × 3 convolutions with 32 channels,
the first with stride two. Three stages then produce C3, C4, and
C5 at strides 4, 8, and 16, with 48, 96, and 128 channels and
depths 3, 4, and 2. A stage block applies a 7 × 7 depthwise
convolution, batch normalization, pointwise expansion by a
factor of 2.5, SiLU activation, pointwise projection, and batch
normalization. Its output is X +γ ⊙F(X), where the channel
scale γ is initialized to 10−5
. This combines large spatial
kernels with narrow learned channel transformations [9]. The
evaluated main configurations do not use structural reparame￾terization.
The top-down neck projects C5 and C4 to 48 channels and
C3 to 32 channels. A refined, bilinearly upsampled feature Uj

C. Context and residual attention

modulation principle of FocalNet [11]. A normalized input

seed, and four gates. Three sequential depthwise 3 × 3 layers

final level also provides a global spatial mean. A softmax
over the four gates weights the three local levels and global
context. A pointwise context transform modulates the query
elementwise, and a projection returns 96 channels through a

D. Grouped BEV and three-dimensional heads
Two task groups predict Car and Pedestrian/Cyclist. Each
group has classification, center offset, log-size, doubled-yaw,
and optional quality branches. Geometric outputs have two
channels per branch and are shared across classes within
a group. The yaw representation (cos 2θ,sin 2θ) decodes as

and width and length are exponentiated from log-size predic￾tions. Gaussian heatmaps provide center supervision [15]; the
radius uses overlap 0.1 and a minimum of four output cells.
Regression targets use nearest-center assignment.
The archived BEV models use independent branches, each
with two 3 × 3 convolutions and a pointwise output layer.
In Final3D, each group shares the first 3 × 3 convolution
while retaining a private second convolution and output layer
per branch. An additional branch predicts LiDAR bottom
height zb and log h. Its masked Smooth-L1 objective, with
coefficient one, is added outside the adaptive BEV task pool.
The two group losses receive equal weights of 0.5. Calibrated
bottom-center transformation and corner projection produce
the camera-coordinate boxes required by the reference evalu￾ator. The model predicts upright yaw-only cuboids and does
not resolve front versus back; orientation similarity is therefore
omitted.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
A. Dataset and training
We use the KITTI object detection data [20], with 3,712
training and 3,769 validation frames. The stored frame lists
are disjoint. Experiments cover Car, Pedestrian, and Cyclist.
Validation supplies both checkpoint selection and the reported
measurements; no hidden KITTI test result is claimed. The
main BEV comparisons complete 50 epochs using seed 42,
batch size 16, BF16 training, AdamW [21], learning rate
7 × 10−4
, weight decay 10−3
, four warmup epochs, and a
cosine schedule to 10−6
. AP is measured every ten epochs,
and the highest Moderate macro-average determines the pri￾mary checkpoint. Final3D completes 100 epochs with eight
warmup epochs and is selected by reference 3D AP. Two older
backbone runs have different budgets and loss-based selection;
their scores are retained only as historical context.
The main configurations use ground-truth object sampling
with probability 0.5, source-relative placement, range scaling
from 0.9 to 1.1, and azimuth jitter of ±5
◦
. Whole-scene quotas
are 15 Cars, 10 Pedestrians, and 10 Cyclists; these are target
counts, rather than a fixed number of insertions. Density adap￾tation, ground checks, static collision rejection, and visibility
protection are enabled. Subsequent transformations apply a
random flip, rotation in [−π/4, π/4], scale in [0.95, 1.05], and
probabilistic translation with standard deviations (0.2, 0.2, 0.1)
m. The configured object database is derived from training
data. Saved configurations and their training-time hashes ac￾company the manuscript.
B. Evaluation protocols
The BEV ablations use a local loader-aligned implemen￾tation of KITTI-style rotated AP with 40 recall positions
(R40). It evaluates the loader’s strictly cropped horizontal
center domain, difficulty filters, and configured neighboring￾class ignores. Car uses IoU 0.7 and Pedestrian/Cyclist use 0.5.
Moderate mAP is the unweighted mean of the three class AP
values, reported as a percentage. This local evaluation supports
internal comparisons, but it does not establish equivalence to
a full-frame reference result.
Final3D uses the unchanged KITTI evaluation and rotated￾overlap code from a pinned OpenPCDet revision [22]. It eval￾uates full-frame original annotations, with calibrated, image￾clipped projections of predicted cuboids. BEV and 3D AP
use the same thresholds and R40 convention. The input
crop remains the detector’s sensing domain, while ground
truth follows the reference evaluator’s full-frame domain. We
report these scores separately from the local BEV study. The
evaluator revision, source hashes, split digest, and checkpoint
digest are supplied in Supplementary Note S3.
C. Efficiency and conditional diagnostics
Saved BEV runtime measurements use PyTorch FP32
on an NVIDIA L4. Model forward time and the of￾fline input-to-detections time are reported separately, in￾cluding loading/encoding, host-to-device transfer, and decod￾ing/suppression. Measurements exclude ten warmup frames
and cover 3,759 timed frames. Parameter counts include the
detector and exclude train-only adaptive criterion parameters.
The Final3D evaluation does not contain stage-level latency
measurements, so the BEV timings are not assigned to it.
Existing CPU diagnostics for the selected Final3D check￾point form one-to-one, same-class associations in descending
BEV IoU, using Moderate ground-truth filters and projected
detection height of at least 25 pixels. At fixed associations,
footprints, scores, and original image boxes, an oracle replaces
the camera-coordinate vertical bottom location and/or height.
The diagnostic counts 3D IoU threshold crossings. It does
not reproduce neighboring-class ignores, DontCare handling,
or AP matching, and therefore does not measure oracle AP.
Its CPU detections also differ slightly from the saved GPU
reference detections.

B. Context, attention, and earlier design choices
Removing focal context from OGA plus IQA lowers mAP
from 70.58% (P4) to 68.72% (P5). However, adding it to the
baseline configuration lowers the score from 68.46% (S32) to
67.38% (P1). Context therefore has a conditional effect within
the available configurations. Among local refinement variants
(Table II), SimAM yields the highest mAP, 70.99%. Its 0.41
pp gain over P4 includes a 1.03 pp Pedestrian improvement
and a small Car reduction. The detail path and ECA produce
lower macro-averages despite small Pedestrian increases.
The earlier runs provide supporting design evidence rather
than a fully controlled sequence. Hist14 scores 50.14% com￾pared with 47.37% for Rich8, and grouped heads score
51.30%, but these runs also differ in code or configuration
details. A more closely aligned width comparison raises mAP
from 66.56% to 67.28% when prediction width increases from
16 to 32 at fusion width 24, adding 114,832 parameters.
Changing fusion width from 24 to 32 at prediction width 16
lowers mAP by 0.31 pp. The apparent 7.91 pp sampling gain
between O32 and H32 accompanies simultaneous changes to
sampling probability, quotas, placement attempts, and valida￾tion batching. Supplementary Tables S2–S5 retain all runs and
identify these comparison limits.
C. Range performance and runtime
P16’s Pedestrian AP in the 30–50 m bin rises from P4’s
4.54% to 9.77%, with 71 rather than 66 true positives among
137 valid objects (Fig. 3b). The absolute score remains low.
The 50–70.4 m bin contains only 13 Pedestrians, limiting
interpretation of its score. These bins expose remaining range
sensitivity rather than establish robust distant-object detection.
P16 uses 852,930 detector parameters and averages 8.55
ms per model forward pass, equivalent to 116.90 forwards/s.
The offline input-to-detections pipeline averages 56.83 ms,
or 17.60 frames/s, with a 95th percentile of 97.37 ms. Its
loading/encoding, transfer, model, and decoding means are
11.74, 6.75, 8.55, and 29.51 ms. Decoding is the largest
recorded stage (Fig. 3a). P3 has a higher model time than P4
but a shorter total pipeline time, illustrating the effect of the
number and distribution of decoded candidates. No embedded￾device throughput is established by these L4 measurements.
D. Reference evaluation of the trained 3D extension
Final3D achieves 71.09% BEV and 57.75% 3D Moderate
mAP under the pinned reference evaluator (Table III). The
detector has 797,382 parameters: 666,769 in the backbone
including the neck, and 130,613 in the grouped heads. This
total is 55,548 parameters below the archived independent￾head P16 BEV model, despite the added vertical outputs. The
models differ in head topology, training duration, checkpoint
criterion, and evaluation protocol, so their AP difference is
not a controlled shared-head ablation. Final3D is selected at
epoch 70; its epoch-100 3D score is 56.47% (Fig. 4a). The
loss-selected epoch-24 checkpoint scores 46.73%, reinforcing
the practical relevance of the declared selection policy.
E. Vertical localization and qualitative evidence
Among 7,130 fixed BEV-matched Cars in the CPU diag￾nostic, 5,743 pass the 3D IoU threshold of 0.7. Replacing
vertical bottom location raises the count to 6,791, whereas
replacing height alone gives 5,873. Joint replacement makes
all 7,130 fixed pairs pass (Fig. 4b). Vertical location correction
recovers 1,048 of the 1,387 failing Car pairs, or 75.6%. Car
bottom-height mean absolute error is 8.66 cm, while signed
bias is only −0.82 cm. The error is thus substantially object￾dependent. Car pass rates decrease from 91.46% within 20
m to 53.29% beyond 40 m among the matched pairs. These
conditional counts identify a vertical localization bottleneck
without predicting the AP gain of an oracle system.

VI. DISCUSSION
The strongest internal evidence is the geometry–quality
interaction. Both OGA and IQA improve the baseline when
used individually, while the combined gain is smaller than their
sum. This pattern is consistent with partial overlap between
improved geometric learning and localization-aware ranking.
Because IQA’s target is derived from the same projection
similarity used in OGA, a shared geometric signal is a
plausible explanation. The experiment does not establish that
explanation causally, and replication across seeds is required
to assess the stability of the observed interaction.
Compactness also involves choices beyond the backbone.
The trained shared-head model remains below 0.8 million
detector parameters while adding vertical regression, but the
saved efficiency evidence covers the independent-head BEV
models. Their runtime decomposition indicates that decoding
and candidate volume can outweigh feature-processing costs.
A deployment study should therefore measure loading, trans￾fer, suppression, and output delivery under a fixed hardware
and precision setting, rather than infer complete-system speed
from model forward frequency.
The 3D results expose a mismatch between footprint-based
confidence and cuboid accuracy. IQA supervises horizontal
geometry, and inference suppresses detections in BEV. Neither
mechanism explicitly rewards vertical overlap. The conditional
diagnostic suggests that object-dependent vertical location
errors are a useful target for future development, especially
for distant Cars. A three-dimensional quality target, vertical-

Several limitations bound the conclusions. The study uses
one dataset, one random seed, and a validation set also used
for checkpoint and variant selection. It lacks a hidden-test
result, independent contemporary baselines under the refer￾ence evaluator, and a matched shared-versus-independent 3D
study. Some historical comparisons change multiple variables.
The local and reference protocols have different ground￾truth domains, and the CPU diagnostic differs from both AP
implementations. The work consequently establishes a com￾pact implementation and useful within-project measurements,
while leaving claims of superiority over modern detectors and
embedded deployment performance to further evaluation.
VII. CONCLUSION
MobilePixorNeXt combines deterministic vertical statistics,
a compact convolutional hierarchy, gated scale fusion, grouped
heads, and oriented geometric training. Local KITTI BEV
ablations show that geometry and separate quality prediction
provide overlapping gains, and that residual SimAM improves
the strongest tested configuration to 70.99% Moderate mAP
with 852,930 detector parameters. A separately trained shared￾head 3D extension has 797,382 parameters and achieves
71.09% BEV and 57.75% 3D Moderate mAP under the pinned
reference evaluator. Conditional diagnostics identify vertical
location as a substantial source of failed Car cuboid overlaps.
These results provide a reproducible basis for broader baseline
comparisons, repeated training runs, and vertical-aware quality
estimation.
